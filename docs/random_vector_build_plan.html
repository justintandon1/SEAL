1What this control has to rule out

§11 of the progress report established the mechanism: on all six benchmarks the budget-limited pile — problems where the baseline hit the 10,000-token cap — supplies at least 100% of the net gain, while the finished pile contributes between −4.2 and +0.6 points. On MATH-500 with S_combo, the 164 capped problems go 1.22% → 51.22% while the 336 finished ones go 95.24% → 93.15%.

So the measured effect is compression, not better reasoning. That is precisely why this control is on the critical path: a norm-57 vector added at layer 20 at every generated position could plausibly shorten chains of thought regardless of which direction it points. Build this control to be decisive, not to be survived.

2The recipe

Recipe: R_iso = 57.06 · g / ‖g‖, where g ~ N(0, I₁₅₃₆) drawn from a pinned seed. Applied at layer 20, coef = −1.0, --remove_bos — identical in every respect to the SEAL arms except the direction.

Three draws, seeds 1 / 2 / 3, written to results/control/R_iso_seed{1,2,3}.pt. Each is a bare 1536-dim float32 tensor plus a sidecar .meta.json, matching what build_combo_vector.py emits — the loader at eval_MATH_steering.py:190 is just torch.load(..., weights_only=True), so no special format is required.

3Decision 1 — why norm 57.06

The intervention is hidden_states[flag, -1] += coef · vector. At a fixed coef = −1.0, the vector's norm is the intervention strength, so a control at the wrong magnitude tests nothing: too small and it is inert by construction, too large and it degrades the model for reasons unrelated to direction.

57.06 is the mean of the three domain-vector norms — the same mean-source target S_combo already uses. Measured from the committed vectors:

The five real vectors span 55.29–59.67, a spread of 8%. Landing the control in the middle of that band means any difference in outcome cannot be attributed to strength.

4Decision 2 — why three draws, not one

MATH-500 already has five SEAL arms, so "do the SEAL vectors separate from the random ones?" can be posed as an exact rank-sum test over the eight arms. The useful property is that the test's best achievable p-value depends only on the group sizes — so it can be computed before a single GPU-hour is spent, and it fixes the minimum useful n.

Where the 0.018 comes from

Take the primary design: five SEAL arms and three random arms, eight arms total, each with one accuracy number. Four steps:

1State the null. The labels carry no information — SEAL and random arms come from the same distribution. If that is true, which three of the eight rank positions the random arms land in is pure chance, and every choice is equally likely.

2Count the layouts. Choosing 3 positions from 8 gives C(8,3) = 56. Under the null each has probability 1/56.

3Find the best case. The most extreme outcome favouring the SEAL vectors is all three random arms ranking below all five SEAL arms. Exactly one of the 56 layouts looks like that.

4Read off the p-value. 1/56 = 0.018 one-sided. In general 1/C(5+n, n).

Why it is a floor. No data can beat maximal separation. If the three random arms land below all five SEAL arms you are at 0.018 with nowhere further to go; any less clean result only pushes p up. So 0.018 is the smallest p-value this design is capable of, whatever the runs return — which is exactly why it can be used to choose n in advance.

The progress report uses a two-sided exact McNemar throughout, so the two-sided column is the one to quote for consistency. It does not change the choice: n = 3 is the first design that can reach significance either way, and n = 2 cannot under the report's own convention.

This is the precise version of "one draw tells you nothing." A single random arm is not merely weak evidence — the design has no configuration that produces a significant separation, so the run cannot answer the question it was commissioned to answer.

Three draws are also adequate for the question actually being asked, which is binary and has an enormous contrast: does a random direction reproduce +12–15, or land near zero? Within-run noise is small (500 paired problems), so each draw is a precise measurement rather than a noisy one. The one-sided 95% t bound at n = 3 is mean + 1.69·sd — if three draws cluster near zero with sd of a point or two, a SEAL-sized effect is excluded by a wide margin.

Three limits to state in the write-up, not discover in review.
  • The rule of three. Zero hits in three draws bounds the fraction of "hot" random directions only at 63%. The claim this run supports is "these three directions did not reproduce the effect", not "random directions generally do not."
  • No resolution in the middle. A consistent +3 with −15% tokens is not separable from zero at n = 3. That outcome gets reported as a partial pass (§10), not rounded to a verdict.
  • The comparison group is not five independent samples. The five SEAL vectors have singular values [2.139, 0.564, 0.325, 0.029, 0.0] — the top direction carries 91.5% of the variance and the effective rank is 1.19. They are close to one direction measured five ways. Charged honestly at an effective m ≈ 2 rather than 5, the floor p at n = 3 rises to 0.10 one-sided (0.20 two-sided) — the same failure mode as n = 1, and the rank-sum framing stops being available. The per-arm McNemar tests against baseline do not depend on m and remain the primary readout.

Five draws would move the floor p to 0.004, keep the rank framing alive at effective m ≈ 2, and tighten the bound 1.77x for two extra arms. Returns fall off after that — 5→10 buys only another 1.64x for five more. Three is the deliberate floor here, not the optimum; see the follow-ons in §11.

Three seeds also settle the sign question for free. A contrast vector has a meaningful orientation — S points from execution toward reflection, and coef = −1.0 pushes against it. A random vector has no such orientation, so "should we also run +1.0?" is not a real question: −R_seed1 is drawn from exactly the same distribution as R_seed2. Three draws at −1.0 already sample both half-spaces.

5Decision 3 — isotropic is the easy control

This is the part most worth arguing about in the writeup. The residual stream is strongly anisotropic: activations concentrate in a low-dimensional region with a few dominant directions. An isotropic Gaussian draw is very nearly orthogonal to that region, so R_iso may come back flat for a reason that has nothing to do with reasoning — it simply never enters the subspace the model computes in.

That does not make R_iso useless. It is the pre-registered spec, it is the standard control in the steering literature, and it is what a reader will ask for first. But its flatness is weak evidence on its own, and the writeup should say so rather than claim more than the design supports.

The harder follow-on: R_cov. Draw from a Gaussian matched to the covariance of layer-20 hidden states at boundary tokens, rescale to 57.06 as above. This lands the control inside the region the model actually occupies, so a flat result there is a genuine statement about direction. It needs the hidden.pt activations, which live on the pod rather than in the repo — which is why it is staged second, not because it matters less.

Recommended order: build and run R_iso first (cheap, expected, pre-registered), and hold R_cov as the immediate follow-on if R_iso is flat. If R_iso is not flat, R_cov becomes unnecessary — the domain story is already in trouble and the budget should go to the coefficient sweep instead.

6The geometry check, and what it can't catch

Before spending GPU time, confirm each draw really is unrelated to the real vectors. In 1536 dimensions that is nearly automatic: the cosine between a random direction and any fixed vector has mean 0 and standard deviation 1/√1536 = 0.0255. Measured over 20,000 draws against S_math: mean +0.0001, sd 0.0251 — theory holds.

So σ ≈ 0.026, and the rule is: reject any draw with |cos| > 0.08 against any of the five vectors. That is about 3σ — roughly a 1-in-500 chance per draw, so across three draws it should essentially never fire. A draw that does exceed it is more likely a bug than bad luck.

Fix the threshold before drawing, not after. The rule is set in advance and any rejection gets recorded in the meta. This matters precisely because it will almost certainly never trigger: a discard rule invented after seeing an inconvenient result is the same rule with none of the credibility.

What this check cannot catch: near-orthogonality to the SEAL vectors does not imply near-orthogonality to whatever direction actually drives compression. If the effect is carried by a generic "wrap up and answer" direction that the SEAL vectors merely overlap with, a random draw could still touch it. That possibility is what the evaluation, not the geometry, has to settle.

7The script

build_random_vector.py mirrors the structure and metadata conventions of build_combo_vector.py. scripts/build_random_vectors.sh wraps it for the three primary draws, screening each seed against the five SEAL vectors and against every earlier seed, so the draws are verified mutually near-orthogonal as they are built. 

# The three primary draws. CPU only, a few seconds.
bash scripts/build_random_vectors.sh

# Equivalent single draw, if you want to see the flags.
python build_random_vector.py \
  --mode iso --seed 1 \
  --like results/general/S_combo.pt \
  --check-against S_combo=results/general/S_combo.pt \
  --check-against S_code=vectors/apps_v_code.pt \
  --check-against S_logic=vectors/logiqa_v_logic.pt \
  --out results/control/R_iso_seed1.pt

8Three things that will bite

Do not reuse check_sign_convention

build_combo_vector.py aborts if any pair of input vectors has a negative cosine — correct there, because summing oppositely-oriented contrast vectors cancels signal. A random vector will sit at cos ≈ 0 and will be negative about half the time. Importing that guard here would reject roughly half of all valid draws, and worse, it would do so silently biasing the control toward the SEAL vectors' half-space. Reuse cosine(), not the guard.

Pin the generator explicitly

Use torch.Generator().manual_seed(s) passed into torch.randn, not the global RNG. Global seeding makes the output depend on whatever else consumed random numbers first, which quietly breaks reproducibility the moment the script grows a second draw. Generate on CPU in float32; the forward pass casts to the activation dtype at modeling_qwen2.py:884.

Normalize before scaling, and check the norm after

A raw randn(1536) has norm ≈ √1536 ≈ 39.2, not 1 and not 57.06. Unit-normalize first, then multiply. Assert the final norm is within 1e-3 of target before writing — a silent factor-of-39 error would look exactly like "random steering destroys the model."

Reproducibility needs the unrounded target norm. The meta records vector_norm rounded to 6 dp for readability, but a rebuild that multiplies by the rounded value lands a few ULPs away and is not bit-identical. The unrounded target_norm is recorded separately and is what a rebuild must use. The rebuild also has to replay any rejected draws — a rejection still advances the generator, so reaching the accepted draw means discarding attempts - 1 first.

10Running it, and the readouts

Revised 2026-08-17: re-split into two phases. The original design below ran three seeds on MATH-500 alone. It is now breadth first, depth second:
  • Phase 1 — R_iso_seed1 across MATH-500, LogiQA and APPS. MATH-500 is done (§11); LogiQA and APPS remain.
  • Phase 2 — seeds 2 and 3, MATH-500 first. Not cancelled, and not optional: §4's floor-p argument means every rank-sum claim waits on the third draw.
The "why one benchmark is enough" reasoning that follows is kept because it still explains why MATH-500 is the right home for phase 2 — but it no longer describes the scope. Read it as the argument for where depth goes, not as a limit on breadth.

Phase 1 is three benchmarks, one seed. Every baseline arm already exists and is reused, so each benchmark costs one new evaluation. MBPP, GSM8K and MMLU stay out of scope — and note run_random_control.sh could not run them anyway, since DATASETS accepts only math,apps,logiqa,livecodebench.

Why MATH-500 still carries the depth

The full grid would be 3 vectors × 6 benchmarks = 18 runs, on the order of the entire ~38 GPU-h already spent on the study — and at the measured 10h53m per arm (§10 budget), far more. Three reasons phase 2 belongs on MATH-500 rather than spread across benchmarks:

Test a control where the effect is strongest. This is the load-bearing argument. MATH-500 has the largest measured gain in the study at +15.0. If a magnitude-matched random direction cannot reproduce the biggest effect, it will not reproduce the small ones — APPS is +1.2 to +2.6, LogiQA +2.6 to +4.0. A pass on the strongest benchmark generalizes downward on its own; a pass on APPS would generalize nowhere, because an arm that moves nothing is unsurprising where almost nothing moves.

The mechanism is most visible there. §11 attributes the gain to recovering baseline-truncated problems, so the control needs a benchmark with enough truncation for that channel to be observable. MATH-500 sits at a 32.8% baseline cap rate with 164 capped and 336 finished problems — both piles large enough to decompose. GSM8K, at 14.9%, would give a capped pile too small to read.

It is the cheapest of the six. APPS runs at batch_size 1 with 8,893 average tokens and an 83.6% cap rate, costing several times a MATH-500 arm. For phase 2 — where three arms are needed and only their spread matters — that cost difference is decisive.

Comparability is free as well: all five SEAL vectors already have MATH-500 coverage on the same rand42_500 selection, so the control arms drop straight into the existing table and the existing paired tests.

Phase 1 order: cheapest first, APPS last. LogiQA before APPS. APPS is several times a MATH-500 arm, and it is also the benchmark whose baseline died at startup on the Aug 15 pod without writing a single prediction — read docs/vast_runbook.md before starting it. Bank the LogiQA result first so phase 1 has two domains on the board even if APPS has to be deferred again.

What still triggers a stop. The original rule stands for its own case: if any arm comes back hot — reproducing the SEAL signature rather than merely moving — stop and treat the domain-specificity claim as unsupported until it is resolved. §11 already records that seed 1 on MATH-500 did not do this.

Report the same five things the SEAL arms report

  1. Accuracy vs baseline, with the exact paired McNemar test
  2. Mean tokens and percent change
  3. Cap-hit rate change
  4. The §11 capped / finished decomposition — the one that actually decides it
  5. Cosine against all five vectors, carried over from the build meta

Wiring: add one arm per benchmark to the BENCHMARKS dict at scripts/report_stats.py:65 pointing at results_for_control_vectors/...; the baseline entries stay untouched. Then python scripts/report_stats.py.

Decision rule, fixed in advance. Call R_iso flat if Δaccuracy is within ±2 points and Δtokens within ±5% on all three seeds. Any seed above +5 points is a live positive result: stop, extend that seed to full benchmark coverage, and treat the domain-specificity claim as unsupported until it is resolved. Anything in between — say a consistent +3 with −15% tokens — is the genuinely awkward case, and it should be reported as a partial pass with the capped/finished split shown, not rounded to either verdict.

On LogiQA the SEAL gains are not individually significant against baseline — S_code is the best at p=0.075, and the rest sit at p=0.20–0.27. That is the small-effect warning in run_random_control.sh playing out: 13–20 problems out of 500. So the claim this arm supports is not "SEAL beats baseline on LogiQA" but "SEAL is clearly distinguishable from a magnitude-matched random direction" (p < 0.005 against all five), which is the question Control A was commissioned to answer.
Against the §10 decision rule, this is not flat. Flat requires Δaccuracy within ±2 points and Δtokens within ±5%. R_iso_seed1 is at −3.6 points and +17.5% tokens — outside both bands, in the negative direction the rule did not name. That is the same "genuinely awkward middle" verdict the MATH-500 arm received, and it is reported as a partial pass with the decomposition shown, not rounded to a verdict. Two benchmarks now agree on the qualitative answer; neither supports the rank-sum framing at n = 1 (§4).

Measured cost, and what it says about the timing dispute

The arm took 7h21m41s for 20 batches — 1325.09 s per batch, and every batch runs the full 10,000 decode steps because 181 of the 500 baseline generations hit the cap, so no batch of 25 can finish early. That is 132.5 ms per decode step, measured on a single pinned GPU with CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES=0 so that device_map="auto" at eval_MATH_steering.py:328 could not pipeline the model across cards.

A single-batch probe run beforehand predicted 1326.81 s/batch — the full arm came in 0.1% off that prediction, confirming that one batch is a legitimate predictor for this workload.

This does not match the 196 ms/step the budget sections are built on. The three figures in the repo, converted to a common unit over the fixed 200,000 decode steps an arm requires:
  • run_random_control.sh — "7.4 GPU-h … A100 PCIe" → 134 ms/step
  • this measurement, A100 SXM4 → 132.5 ms/step
  • §10 and docs/aws_runbook.md — "10h53m on g6e.xlarge" → 196 ms/step
The first two agree to within 1%. The third is 48% higher. Either the seed-1 MATH-500 arm did not take 10h53m, or an A100 is ~32% faster than an L40S here — and the second reading would contradict the "a faster GPU does not buy a shorter run" claim in §10 and the runbook. This document does not resolve which; it records that the two A100 figures agree and the L40S figure does not.
VRAM: ~50 GB steady, 63.8 GB peak reserved — not the ~21 GB the runbook predicts. docs/aws_runbook.md budgets 6.6 GB of fp32 weights plus a 13.8 GB KV cache and concludes "24 GB is enough." Sampled through this arm, nvidia-smi reported 44–64 GB in use. Some of that is PyTorch's caching allocator rather than live tensors, but the working set is far above 24 GB, so this configuration cannot run on a 24 GB card at batch 25 with a 10,000-token budget. The 4090 claim in the Vast runbook cannot be right at these settings.

14The batch cross-check

The §12 caveat in the progress report — S_general's APPS arm ran at APPS_BATCH_SIZE=20 against everything else's batch 1, "should not change results under greedy decoding, but not empirically cross-checked" — is now checked. The first 20 problems of the APPS selection were regenerated in one batch of 20 on the same box, same weights, same steering as the batch-1 arm, and diffed character-for-character:

20/20 outputs identical. Roughly 200,000 generated tokens, not one differs. Batching does not change greedy fp32 outputs in this codebase on same-model, same-box conditions. The S_general asterisk is resolved, and batched suites (~6× cheaper on APPS-class benchmarks) are validated for future runs.

The same night produced the sharp contrast: cross-environment reproduction is not exact — re-generating 7B baseline examples on a different card/torch build produced 0/44 string-identical generations (accuracy shifts only ~1 point on an 84-example paired probe, but token streams diverge). Batching is safe; changing machines is the thing that moves outputs.

16What this does and doesn't answer

It answers: whether a magnitude-matched perturbation at the same site and coefficient reproduces the compression and the cap-recovery gain. That is the load-bearing question for every domain-specificity claim in the report.

It does not answer whether the SEAL recipe is reasoning-specific. A random direction and a direction built by the same contrast procedure on non-reasoning text are different objects, and the second is Control B. Nor does it speak to whether the boundary labels carry the signal — that is the shuffled-label control from the research plan, which is arguably the sharpest of the three and is still unscheduled.

The pre-registered interpretation grid stands: both controls flat with the SEAL vectors at +12–15 means reasoning-specific; B gains while A stays flat means the recipe generalizes; both gaining means the effect is a generic length-and-layer artifact and the domain story collapses.
