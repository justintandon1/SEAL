Five steering vectors, one direction: general vs. task-specific reasoning calibration

Three domain-specific vectors (math, code, logic) and two general ones (pooled and combined), all applied unchanged at layer 20 with coefficient α = −1.0 on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, across six benchmarks. All five are close to the same direction in activation space, all five produce statistically indistinguishable effects, and all five cut reasoning tokens by 7.5–52% depending on the benchmark.

Model DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B Site layer 20 · coef −1.0 Decoding greedy · max_tokens 10,000 Benchmarks MATH-500 · LogiQA · APPS · MBPP · GSM8K · MMLU Date 2026-08-07
Result: every vector improves accuracy and cuts tokens on every benchmark it has been run on. On MATH-500 all five lift a 64.4% baseline to 76.8–79.4% (all p < 1e−9 paired), and no two steered vectors differ significantly from each other (all p ≥ 0.15). The geometry predicted this — the domain vectors are close to rank-1, 86.7% of variance in one direction — and the benchmarks confirm it. But across all six benchmarks the gain comes almost entirely from problems where the baseline exhausted its token budget, which is why the queued controls are the critical path rather than a formality.

Finding 1 — every vector helps, on every benchmark, and shortens the CoT On MATH-500 all five vectors lift accuracy from a 64.4% baseline to 76.8–79.4% (+12.4 to +15.0 points), every one significant on a paired McNemar test (p from 1e−9 to 2e−14). The direction of effect is positive on all six benchmarks, and average generated tokens fall everywhere — from −7.5% on APPS to −51.7% on GSM8K.
Finding 2 — the five vectors are not distinguishable from each other All 10 steered-vs-steered pairings on MATH-500 are non-significant (p ≥ 0.15), and the five arms agree on the outcome of 376 / 500 problems — including agreeing on which 54 none of them can solve. Same story on LogiQA (all pairs p ≥ 0.46) and APPS, now at full five-vector coverage like MATH-500 and LogiQA (p ≥ 0.39, all five arms agreeing on 410/500). The geometry predicted this: the domain vectors are close to rank-1, so pooled, combined, and individual vectors are all close to the same direction. The benchmarks confirm the geometry.
Finding 3 — the gain comes from problems where the baseline ran out of token budget

New analysis for this report, measured with the model tokenizer on every arm of every benchmark. Splitting each benchmark by whether the baseline hit the 10,000-token cap, the already-finishing half contributes between −4.2 and +0.6 points — while the cap-hit half contributes the entire net gain. On MATH-500, S_combo's +15.0 decomposes into +16.4 from the capped third and −1.4 from the rest.

Steering makes the model terser, which converts “never finished thinking” into “produced an answer.” It is not yet demonstrated that it improves reasoning quality — on problems the model could already finish, it usually costs a little. This is exactly why the mentor-requested controls matter, and it raises the prior that they will reproduce a chunk of the effect.

03How the five vectors were built

The shared SEAL recipe

All five vectors are the same kind of object: a contrast between two piles of hidden states, collected at reasoning-step boundaries inside the model's own chain of thought.

  1. Generate traces. Run the model greedily on a training split and keep 500 correct + 500 incorrect traces (both piles, so the vector is not a correctness direction).
  2. Find step boundaries. Every vocabulary token containing \n\n, restricted to positions inside <think>. Record the layer-20 hidden state at each such position.
  3. Tag each boundary by the keywords that open the following step: reflection (“wait”, “let me check”, “hmm”, …), transition (“alternatively”, “another approach”, …), or execution (everything else).
  4. Contrast the piles.
S = mean(H_reflection ∪ H_transition) − mean(H_execution)

Applied with coef = −1.0, i.e. the model is pushed away from the reflection/transition direction and toward execution. That is the SEAL mechanism: suppress non-essential reflective detours in the CoT. Full walkthrough in the SEAL pipeline study note and the layer-data explainer.

Boundary counts are the number of \n\n positions falling in each pile. LogiQA contributes ~8× fewer boundaries than the other two because its chains of thought are much shorter — this matters for S_general below.

S_general — pooled across domains

S_general does not average the three finished vectors. It goes back to the raw boundary activations, pools every labelled boundary from all three domains into one pair of piles, and takes a single contrast:

S_general = mean(all check ∪ switch, all domains) − mean(all other, all domains)

This is the Phase 1 (count-weighted) build: each domain casts as many votes as it has boundaries, so domains with longer CoTs dominate. Pooled counts: 137,452 reflection/transition rows against 228,672 execution rows, out of 366,124 total. Resulting norm 55.78.

Because the pools are not domain-balanced, MATH and APPS together supply 93.6% of the rows and LogiQA only 6.4% — which is why S_general ends up much closer to S_math (cos 0.969) and S_code (cos 0.975) than to S_logic (cos 0.797). A domain-balanced Phase 2 build (subsample each domain to min(n_domain)) is implemented behind --balance but has not been run. Both phases are specified in the S_general build plan, which walks through the domain sources, the boundary budget, and how the three keyword sets are mixed.

S_combo — combined from the finished vectors

S_combo is the arithmetic control for S_general: if pooling raw activations is doing something a simple weighted sum cannot, S_general and S_combo should behave differently.

S_combo = scale · ( a·Ŝ_math + b·Ŝ_code + c·Ŝ_logic ), a = b = c = 1

Three build decisions, each with a reason:

  • Unit-normalize each source first. Nearly free here (the three norms are within 8% of each other, so cos(raw sum, unit sum) = 0.99996) but it keeps the build correct if a future vector arrives with a very different magnitude.
  • Equal weights. The three sources are close to rank-1, so reweighting cannot rotate the sum much — across a 4× weight range the direction stays within cos 0.985 of equal weights. Grid-searching weights would spend GPU budget resolving ~0.015 of cosine. Equal weights is also the correct comparison object: a domain-balanced S_general equals the average of the domain vectors, so this tests pooling vs. combining rather than tuned vs. untuned.
  • Rescale to the mean source norm (57.06). The unit sum has norm 2.79 against ~57 for the domain vectors; saved unscaled it would be a ~20× weaker intervention at the same coefficient and would look inert. Scaling never changes direction, and the factor (20.43×) is recorded in the meta.

04Geometry: are they the same direction?

Largely, yes. Pairwise cosine between the vectors we hold on disk:

cos(S_general, S_combo) = 0.981 — the highest off-diagonal value in the table. This is the most direct test of “is pooling raw activations the same as summing the finished vectors,” and at 0.981 the answer is essentially yes: pooling and combining land almost on top of each other. Computed once both .pt files were committed to the repo (previously S_general's vector existed only as a .meta.json) — see §13.

Stacking the three domain vectors and taking the SVD gives singular values [1.613, 0.541, 0.325]. The top direction carries 86.7% of the squared variance: the three domain vectors are mostly one direction plus small domain-specific residuals. That is the structural reason to expect all five vectors to do roughly the same thing at the same layer and coefficient.

The one genuine outlier is S_logic, which sits at cos 0.75–0.80 from the other two. It is also the vector built from by far the fewest boundaries (9,683 vs 51k and 76k) and from the shortest chains of thought.

Are the five vectors different from each other?

No — and because this is the central claim of the report, here is exactly how that is tested.

Why not just compare the two accuracies?

S_combo scores 79.4% and S_code 76.8%. The tempting move is to compare those against their ±3.5-point confidence intervals, see that the intervals overlap, and call it a draw. That test is valid but weak: it treats the two runs as if they were independent samples of different problems. They are not — both arms answered the same 500 problems, and they agree on 86% of them. Throwing away that pairing wastes most of the information.

What we do instead: McNemar's exact test

Compare the two arms problem by problem. Every problem falls into one of four cells:

Problem outcomeCount (S_code vs S_combo)Informative?
Both arms correct356no — tells us nothing about a difference
Both arms wrong75no — same
S_code right, S_combo wrong  (b)28yes
S_code wrong, S_combo right  (c)41yes

The two agreeing cells cancel out — a problem both arms solve, or neither solves, carries no evidence that one vector is better. Only the 69 disagreements do.

If the two vectors were genuinely equivalent, then on each of those 69 problems it should be a coin flip which arm happens to get it. So under the null hypothesis b ~ Binomial(b + c, 0.5), and the p-value is the exact two-sided binomial tail:

p = 2 × P( X ≤ min(b, c) ), X ~ Binomial(b + c, 0.5) S_code vs S_combo: b = 28, c = 41, b + c = 69 p = 2 × P(X ≤ 28) = 0.148

Reading that: if the two vectors really behaved identically, a split at least as lopsided as 28–41 would happen about 15% of the time by chance alone. That is far too common to call a real difference. The same computation for every other pair lands even further from significance.

Three notes on the method.
  • We use the exact binomial tail rather than the usual χ² approximation, because with 49–69 discordant pairs the approximation is not reliable at the edges. Implemented as mcnemar_exact() in scripts/report_stats.py.
  • No multiple-comparison correction is applied across the 10 pairs. It would only make these p-values larger, i.e. push them further from significance, so omitting it is the conservative choice for the claim being made.
  • Non-significant is not the same as proven identical. This test says we cannot distinguish the vectors at n = 500, not that they are exactly equal. With enough problems a 1–2 point difference would eventually resolve. The claim is that any difference is small relative to what this evaluation can detect — which is precisely what the near rank-1 geometry predicted.

12What is actually driving the gain

The headline is “+15 points on MATH-500.” This section asks which problems those points came from, and the answer turns out to be narrow enough to change how the result should be read.

The idea: sort the problems by what the baseline did, then score both arms on each pile

Every run has a hard limit of 10,000 generated tokens. A reasoning model that keeps second-guessing itself can spend all 10,000 still inside <think> and get cut off before it ever writes an answer. That is not a wrong answer — it is no answer, and it scores zero automatically.

So we split the problem set in two, using only the baseline run:

  1. Ran out of budget. The baseline generation reached the 10,000-token limit. On MATH-500 this is 164 of the 500 problems (32.8%).
  2. Finished. The baseline stopped on its own, inside budget. The remaining 336 problems.

The split is decided entirely by the baseline, then held fixed while we score every steered arm on the same two piles. So it is a like-for-like comparison of the same problems, not a re-partition per arm.

Worked example: MATH-500, baseline vs. S_combo

Why isn't the 1.2% just zero? It should be. A generation cut off mid-reasoning never wrote an answer, so there is nothing for the grader to score. We inspected the 2 problems it credited: neither contains a \boxed{}, neither closed </think>, and neither contains the phrase “the answer is” or “final answer”. Both simply stop mid-sentence, one of them mid-multiplication.

They score because the MATH extractor has no “no answer stated → wrong” rule. When it finds no answer marker it falls back to the raw generation text, and for these two a number matching the gold answer was recovered out of the truncated reasoning. They are grader false positives, not solved problems — the same failure mode as the LogiQA extractor we replaced, which guessed a letter from unfinished traces.

So the honest reading of that cell is 0 / 164 = 0.0%. Scoring those two as wrong would move the baseline from 64.4% to 64.0% and S_combo's gain from +15.0 to +15.4 — it slightly strengthens the effect, so the tables throughout this report keep the committed 64.4% as the conservative number. See §13.

The same near-zero appears on GSM8K, MBPP, LogiQA and APPS — 0.0–1.2% in every case. MMLU is the one real exception at 23.1%, because it is 4-way multiple choice and its grader can still pull a letter out of a truncated generation, landing near the 25% chance floor.

And the 95.2%? On the 336 problems the baseline did finish, it was right 95.2% of the time. That is the real ceiling: when this model gets to state an answer on a MATH-500 problem, it is usually right. Its 64.4% overall score is not a reasoning ceiling — it is 95.2% on two thirds of the set, dragged down by ~0% on the third that never finished.

Now split S_combo's +15.0 points by where they were earned. Each pile's contribution is its accuracy change times its share of the problem set:

ran-out pile: (51.22% − 1.22%) × 164/500 = +50.00 × 0.328 = +16.4 pts finished pile: (93.15% − 95.24%) × 336/500 = −2.08 × 0.672 = −1.4 pts net = +15.0 pts

So steering did not make the model a better reasoner on the problems it could already handle — there it lost 2 points. What it did was stop the model from talking itself past the budget on the other third, turning guaranteed zeros into roughly coin flips. All +15 points, and then some, come from that one pile.

The honest statement of the result

Steering makes the model terser. On problems where the baseline was thinking itself past the token cap, that converts a guaranteed zero into a partial success. On problems the baseline could already finish, the same terseness usually costs a little. The net is positive everywhere, but because a large slice of every eval set is budget-limited — from 14.9% on GSM8K to 83.6% on APPS.

This is consistent with SEAL's own account of the mechanism — suppressing non-essential reflection — but it means the headline numbers are not yet evidence that the vector improves reasoning quality. Distinguishing “makes it shorter” from “makes it better” is precisely what the queued controls are for.

It also explains our low MATH-500 baseline. 64.4% sits well below the commonly cited 83.9% for this model, and the gap is a budget/protocol difference, not a bug: the reported figure uses a 32,768-token generation limit with sampling, while our runs are greedy at 10,000 tokens. A third of our baseline generations truncate; at 32k far fewer would, and the headroom steering is exploiting would shrink accordingly. The 7B result (+3.4 at an 84.4% baseline) is the same effect seen from the other side.

13Caveats and known gaps

  1. No controls yet. The biggest gap when this report was written; the first control has since run — see §9. Nothing here rules out that any intervention of this norm at this layer shortens the CoT and produces the same gain.
  2. Single greedy sample, 10,000-token budget. No sampling variance is measured, and a large fraction of every baseline set is budget-limited. Our 64.4% vs the commonly reported 83.9% is explained by this, but it means our headroom is partly an artifact of the protocol.
  3. Coverage is uneven (§5). S_general and S_combo now both cover MATH-500, LogiQA and APPS. MBPP, GSM8K and MMLU still have no general-vector rows at all.
  4. The S_general APPS run used a different batch size than the other four arms. APPS_BATCH_SIZE=20 vs the default of 1 used for baseline, S_math, S_code, S_logic and S_combo. The steering hook applies per-sequence (each row of the batch is tracked and steered independently at its own <think> boundaries), and MATH-500/LogiQA already run at batch 25 without issue, so this should not change results under greedy decoding — but it was not empirically cross-checked against batch 1 on APPS specifically before this run.
  5. S_general is Phase 1 only (count-weighted). The domain-balanced Phase 2 build is implemented but unrun, so “pooled” currently means “pooled and dominated by MATH+APPS.”
  6. 7B token counts not computed — that model uses a different tokenizer, so its rows carry accuracy only.
  7. The MATH grader credits some answerless generations. With data_name="omni-math", extract_answer has no “no answer stated → wrong” rule: when a generation contains no \boxed{} and no “the answer is” phrase, it falls back to the raw text. On the MATH-500 baseline this credits 2 truncated generations that never stated an answer (§12). The effect on the headline is small and in the conservative direction — correcting it would raise S_combo's gain from +15.0 to +15.4 — but the fallback is the same failure mode as the LogiQA extractor we replaced, and the MATH/GSM8K/MMLU results deserve the same audit.
  8. Coefficient is fixed at −1.0 and layer at 20. No sweep. All conclusions are conditional on that operating point.

14Next steps: controls and ablations

The findings above make the controls the critical path, not a nicety. If the effect is “terser CoT recovers truncated answers,” then interventions with nothing to do with reasoning may reproduce it — and we need to know that before claiming a reasoning-specific direction.

Priority 1 — the two controls, same 500 problems, same harness

Done

A. Norm-matched random vector

Run as R_iso_seed1 on MATH-500, LogiQA and APPS — see §9. A random direction in 1536-d at the mean-source norm 57.06, layer 20, coef −1.0. The answer: no — the random direction lengthens the CoT on all three benchmarks and loses 2.4–3.6 points (significant on APPS, p = 0.006), the mirror image of the SEAL arms. Seeds 2–3 remain queued: the rank-sum framing needs three draws.

Queued

B. Null-keyword vector from non-reasoning text

Run the full extraction recipe — generate traces, tag \n\n boundaries by the same reflection/transition keywords, contrast the piles — on a non-reasoning corpus, then evaluate on MATH-500. What changes is the extraction source; the evaluation benchmark stays MATH-500. Answers: does the recipe find something reasoning-specific, or does the same keyword contrast on any text produce a vector that helps math just as much?

Interpretation grid, decided in advance
  • Both controls flat, our vectors gain +12–15 → the shared direction is reasoning-specific. Strongest outcome.
  • Control B gains but A is flat → the recipe generalizes but is not reasoning-specific; the keyword contrast finds a generic “stop hedging, commit” direction in any text.
  • Both controls gain → the effect is a generic length/decoding intervention at this layer and coefficient, and the domain story collapses. Given §11, this is a live possibility and we should say so up front rather than discover it later.

In all three cases we should report the token savings and the cap-hit decomposition from §11 for the control arms, since that is where the signal lives.
